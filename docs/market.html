First, the Blocking Issue: Borrowed IP

Must resolve before any commercial or public use

Orsted and Ghislaine are characters from Mushoku Tensei (Rifujin na Magonote; licensed novels, manga, and anime). This isn't a case of archetype resemblance — the names, visual designs (Orsted: white hair, golden eyes, oppressive aura, "Dragon God," #1 ranked being; Ghislaine: brown-skinned swordswoman beastfolk, "Sword God"-style ranking), and power-cosmology roles are directly recognizable. As private fanfiction this is perfectly fine. For querying agents, paid serialization (Royal Road → Kindle Unlimited pipelines, Patreon), or self-publishing, it is a hard blocker: it exposes the work to takedowns and, more practically, signals "fanfic" to any acquiring professional within one page of those characters appearing.

The good news: the surgery is shallow. Both characters are structurally original to this story — their plot functions (the untouchable #1 who chose to protect commoners; the joyful predator who teaches Carina the lesson) don't depend on the source material at all. Rename them, change the visual signatures (drop the dragon-god framing for, say, a primordial title native to this world's Gaia cosmology — the setting already has four primordial dragons to hang it on), and adjust two or three descriptive paragraphs. Estimated effort: a day. Everything else they do can stay.

What's fine: the 72 Lords names (Ars Goetia — public domain demonology, used by dozens of franchises); Billy Kidd/Calamity Jane (historical figures, public domain, and clearly transformed); murim/wuxia conventions (Mount Hua, Heavenly Demon, Orthodox/Unorthodox sects are genre furniture across hundreds of works, like "elves"); Wendigo (folklore); the dragon names' Skyrim-flavored phonemes (style echo, not identity — defensible, though consider differentiating); "Plum Blossom" sword arts (genre-standard Mount Hua iconography). The specific named characters are the only legal/positioning problem found in 226k words.

Genre Placement & Audience

Primary shelf: adult dark epic fantasy with survival elements and East-meets-West power systems. It sits at the intersection of three healthy readerships:

  • Character-first dark fantasy readers (the audience that made The First Law, Kings of the Wyld, and The Sword of Kaigen work): the found-family-to-tragedy arc, morally contested violence, and banter-forward ensemble are squarely for them.
  • Progression/cultivation crossover readers (Cradle, Mother of Learning, murim manhwa audiences): ki systems, ranked powers, sect politics, and fight-scene rigor speak fluent progression — though note, this is not a progression story structurally (power-ups are rare and costly), which is a positioning choice to make explicitly.
  • Anime/manhwa-literate web fiction readers (Royal Road's core): tonal range, named techniques, the Tablet rankings, and arc-based structure map naturally onto serial reading.

Age positioning: adult (not YA, despite a 19-year-old lead — the Billy material, the trials, and the violence place it firmly adult; do not let anyone package this as YA). Reader promise: "a found family forged in a frozen apocalypse — and what it costs to keep one." The Book Two ending means marketing must frame the series as a long-arc tragedy-with-hope, not a cozy adventure, or reviews will punish the mismatch.

The Sellable Hooks

  1. The blind prophet. Muse is the marketing department's gift: a genuinely differentiated protagonist whose disability is craft, not gimmick, with a one-line pitch ("a blind prophet whose god has gone silent performs faith for the last believer"). The blind-surgery chapter is an excerptable showpiece.
  2. The premise compression: "A nation sealed under a dome of endless winter; outside, the world argues — inside, five strangers walk toward the cold's heart." Clean, visual, posterable.
  3. The campfire promise. "Five wishes for when the dome breaks" is a ready-made fandom artifact (art prompts, epigraphs, end-of-book gut punch) and the series' emotional brand.
  4. The Gallows Horn and the knock. The monster design and the horror beats are shareable in a way generic undead are not — lead with them in serial marketing.
  5. Villains with a group chat. The Lords' cold war (Dantalion/Elizabeth/Barbatos/the Merchant) gives the series a second fandom-engine — "the Dantalion family dinner" energy travels well on social.
  6. Carina. The character readers will fight about, write essays about, and come back for. Tragic-arc leads with comic surfaces are fandom accelerant.

Length, Format & Series Math

Book One ~93k words (right in adult-fantasy debut range, 90–120k); Book Two ~133k (normal for a second volume). Chapter lengths (2–4.5k) are serial-perfect. The series is visibly planned long (the unspent-mystery ledger supports 4–6 volumes). Two format implications:

  • If pursuing traditional publishing: Book One must stand alone better (see structural notes) — agents evaluate volume one as a complete experience. The Valdara arc gives you a self-contained spine; the revision is about making ch. 30 a landing instead of a ramp.
  • If pursuing serial/indie: the current structure is already close to ideal; the Book One "non-ending" is a normal arc boundary in serial format. The bigger serial consideration is cadence — this prose density supports 2–3 chapters/week sustainably only with a backlog.

Publication Paths, Ranked

1. Web serial first (Royal Road → Patreon → Kindle Unlimited) — best fit

The format matches the structure; the audience matches the influences; the murim/dungeon/ranking vocabulary is native there; and the found-family-betrayal arc is proven serial fuel. Requirements: the IP fix, the copyedit pass, a 20+ chapter launch backlog, and a cover/title package that signals "dark survival fantasy," not LitRPG (no stat screens — don't attract the wrong readers with bracket-spell styling). Realistic outcome band: this writing quality with consistent posting typically builds a real audience; the campfire/dissolution arcs are the kind that generate word-of-mouth spikes.

2. Self-publish as novels (after structural revision)

Viable — Sword of Kaigen lane. Needs the Book One ending fix more than the serial path does, professional cover/blurb, and a marketing plan built on the hooks above. Higher upfront cost, more control, slower discovery without the serial on-ramp.

3. Traditional querying

Possible after revision — the character work and the Valdara arc are genuinely query-competitive — but this manuscript's strengths (long-arc ensemble tragedy, two-book payoff structures, genre-blend vocabulary) are better rewarded by readers directly than by a query funnel that will judge Book One's ending and the crowded dark-fantasy debut market. If queried: lead with Muse, comp as above, and have Book One's revised ending in place first.

Title, Branding & Packaging

"Permafrost" is a strong series title — thematically perfect (see the analysis page: everything in this story is preserved-frozen, waiting to thaw into life or rot) and memorable. Two cautions: it's used by other works across media (not blocking, but check your subtitle space), and individual volumes need their own titles for series shelf-logic. Suggestions that fit the existing architecture: Book One Permafrost: The Long Cold or The Prophet's Winter; Book Two The Thaw That Didn't Come / What the Cold Keeps. Chapter titles are recommended generally — this manuscript's chapters have natural names (the review pages here demonstrate it) and serial readers navigate by them.

Cover direction: the Hallowed Ground image (golden cathedral of light inside black fog, the eye above it) or the five-around-a-campfire-under-the-dome composition. Avoid generic hooded-figure dark fantasy; this book's differentiators are visual.

Blurb spine (drafted): "The dome fell, and the winter never left. Two hundred people are starving in Velldale, a goddess has gone silent, and the five strangers who answer the call — a blind prophet, a paladin who cannot die, a craftsman who counts everything, an exile who keeps the souls she takes, and a girl raised to be a weapon — will cross a frozen nation to find out why. What they find will cost them each other."

Pitch Materials (Drafted)

Logline

Under a dome of endless winter, a blind prophet who has lost her god leads four broken strangers across a frozen nation — and the family they build on the road is the first thing the cold takes back.

One-paragraph query pitch

When the dome sealed Kordova in apocalyptic winter, Muse woke from a thousand-year sleep into a world that had erased her faith — and a goddess who no longer answers. To keep the last believer beside her from breaking, she lies: small fragments of false prophecy, nightly, to a paladin who survived ten centuries of dying in the dark on faith alone. The mission that follows — a starving village, a gilded lord feasting over a plague city, a thing in the blizzard that knocks politely on cabin doors — forges five strangers into a family and then asks what that family is willing to kill for, lie about, and walk away from. PERMAFROST is an adult dark fantasy (93,000 words, first of a planned series) that crosses the martial-arts cosmology of The Sword of Kaigen with the found-family warmth and moral teeth of Kings of the Wyld.

Series promise (for serial blurbs)

Five wishes were made around a campfire, to be kept when the dome breaks: a drink, a fight, a prayer, a grave, the sun. The series is the story of what it costs to keep them.

Bottom Line

This is a commercially viable series wearing a draft's clothes. The retention engines that actually sell long fantasy — characters readers adopt, chapters that end on hooks, fights with ideas, and emotional payoffs that hit — are all present at a professional level. The blockers are mechanical: two renamed characters, one copyedit, one ending. The recommended sequence: (1) IP fix and copyedit, (2) Book One capstone restructure, (3) launch serial with a deep backlog while drafting Book Three — because the single biggest market risk of this saga is ending Book Two on "Make me numb. Ruin me." and making readers wait. Don't make them wait.
