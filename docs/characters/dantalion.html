Dantalion / Everos

The man who accidentally froze a country, deliberately arranged his own murder, and considers both outcomes acceptable — the dome's author and the series' most entertaining mind.

Role: Inciting villain; Uthesia's target; Elizabeth's father; now free agent lich Origin: Lowest-ranked Dantalion in bloodline history, by choice Signature: Golden curls, the insufferable cadence, necromancy, the pocket watch Grade: A

Who He Is

Dantalion is the cause of everything — the artifact he activated without reading the manual is the dome, the winter, every frozen corpse in seventy chapters — and the manuscript's boldest characterization choice is to render the author of the apocalypse as a domestic comedy. He is introduced mid-bicker with his fourteen-year-old daughter about experimental methodology, while looting a royal vault, before resurrecting his mother-in-law so his wife can murder her personally ("Then next time, let me kill my own mother. You know how long I waited for that"). The family scenes — hair-brushing during siege command, performance reviews of his own parenting, "I love you" / "I love you too, Dad" delivered around a knife — are horrifying precisely because the affection is real. This is the Addams Family staffed by war criminals, and it gives the villain plot a watchability that grim scheming alone never achieves.

Under the comedy is a coherent psychology: a brilliant man born into obligations he never wanted, who chose indolence as rebellion, was punished for it by his peers for centuries, and whose every catastrophic act traces to delegating diligence ("We'll deal with it when it happens" — the Dantalion motto, as Elizabeth says). The dome itself is characterization: the disaster of a man who deploys an artifact he didn't research because researching is effort.

Arc & the Everos Turn

His Book Two run is a masterclass in villain plotting that stays character-first: the Yoo manipulation (beating the strongest person in the story with her own culture's reverence for Vassago), the Barbatos bluff (defeating a superior mind by feeding it true premises and letting it build the lie itself — then explaining the trick to Elizabeth as a lesson, which is both exposition and parenting), the Ostervik siege as a chess sacrifice to empty Solarspire, and then the reveal that reframes everything: he engineered his own assassination. The succession pact extracted under the Merchant's terms, the Soul Gem dangled where Elizabeth could weaponize it, the death accepted with "I love you too, Dad" — and the resurrection as the lich Everos, hairless, gray, delighted, with the thesis statement: "As a Lord, my failures were her inheritance. As a lich, my enemies are my own." His petty-revenge manifesto ("I'll take a few heads… exactly why they should have left me alone") promises a Book Three villain unshackled from every constraint that made him manageable.

The reveal also retroactively deepens the Uthesia thread: "Out of everyone I've wronged, she deserved it the most" — delivered posthumously through Elizabeth to a paralyzed Uthesia — is either a confession, a taunt, or a riddle, and the series is clearly saving the answer. Good. Make it devastating.

Critique

  • The engineered-death reveal slightly over-credits him. "Elizabeth was always going to kill me… so I created a situation where she felt justified" requires him to have predicted the Merchant's exact terms and Elizabeth's exact patience. The text can keep the swagger while admitting margin — Everos privately acknowledging the plan had a real chance of just being death would make him smarter, not weaker.
  • His necromantic capacity needs bounds. Armies "collected from the dungeon break," personal undead screening the north, knights, mirrors, remote totems — the inventory grows on demand. Lichdom is the moment to publish his new rulebook.
  • The wife, Talia, is a prop with one (great) scene. She's now a loose thread with a title-succession grievance — either develop or account for her.
  • Watch the charm budget: with Barbatos down, Everos and Elizabeth are both witty schemers; B3 risks villain-voice monoculture. Differentiate registers (his warmth vs. her precision is the existing, correct axis — keep them on-page together rarely so each stays distinct).

Verdict — A. The dome's author, the books' comic engine, and the rare villain whose defeat promotes him. The Everos turn is the single best plot twist in the series because it's the only one that is simultaneously a surprise, a character study, and a sequel engine. Bound his powers and mind the over-crediting, and he'll carry the antagonist half of Book Three.
