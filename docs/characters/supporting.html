The Supporting Cast

Focused analyses of every secondary character with plot weight. One-scene functionaries (gate guards, the Salt Barrel barkeep, Count Dravemont's war council) are noted only where they matter.

Fafnir — Head Chaptermaster, Kordova

The best bureaucrat in modern fantasy drafting. Fafnir's job is to make institutions dramatic, and he does it through accumulating small, perfect beats: the forged-tag amnesty ("Times like these don't make me picky about who's helping"), the party-dissolution paperwork as grief liturgy, kneeling to Lords he's simultaneously outmaneuvering, the verbal fencing with Barbatos where deference is weaponized ("As you wish, my Lord" — agreement as insult), and refusing Yoo — the strongest person on the continent — on a point of member privacy. He's also the series' quiet detective: the Green Alkanet connection, the Elizabeth interview where finding no seams is the alarm. Critique: he's so reliably wise that he risks becoming the story's answer key; give him one consequential misjudgment (arguably sending Yoo to Ostervik is it — develop that). Grade: A−.

Ghislaine — Sword God, Rank 4

The joyful apex predator: hanging upside-down in war councils, howling from the Solarspire, offering jerky as diplomacy — and then the series' most morally complicated act, executing Carina as pedagogy and resurrecting her with one of the Guild's last scrolls ("Let this be your rebirth"). The lesson-kill scene is conceptually brilliant (her prediction-illusions weaponize Carina's own skill) and the fallout is honest (Fafnir's fury; Carina's trauma). Her scent-based epistemology — identifying the party by Orsted's touch, "Part of your pack?" — gives the world texture no other character can provide. Critique: she is also, with Orsted, the manuscript's critical legal problem — name, design, and role recognizably derive from an existing licensed property (see Market). The character function is fully salvageable with a redesign. Grade: B+ as written; A-potential post-redesign.

Orsted — Dragon God, Rank 1

The setting's load-bearing monolith: the unconquerable #1 whose presence reorganizes rooms, who walks through warship hulls, and whose restraint (the valley calculus about not killing Barbatos — "humans don't have a thousand years") gives the power ceiling a conscience. His ch. 10 scene with Muse — the anonymous confession to a blind priestess, "one day, even I will die. And when I do, the world will still be safe. Because of people like you" — is the series' thematic keystone, and his childhood connection to Muse (the giants at the temple, a thousand years ago) is an elegant bridge between the buried past and the present. Critique: same IP problem as Ghislaine, and the same solution; also, the series must guard his screen time ruthlessly — every Orsted appearance resets the stakes ceiling. Grade: A− as a function; rename/redesign required.

Stonewalker — Machine God, Rank 10

The underestimated tenth seat, and Book Two's best surprise: the comic-relief engineer ("reasons the dwarf isn't allowed to blow things up") who, in ch. 38, delivers the smartest fight in the series — baiting Uthesia's necromancer act with a silently-toggled antimagic field, shooting a shadow assassin over his shoulder, and monologuing the deduction like a detective. His "weakest of the Ten is not the insult you think it is" speech converts a running joke into a credo. The infrastructure montage (heating plates, workshops) also makes him the only Ten member whose power is civic. Critique: his warlord history is raised and dropped in one paragraph — that's a B3 invoice; and his assignment as Elizabeth's guard makes him an unwitting asset of the most dangerous person in the city, which the text should eventually make him notice. Grade: A−.

Leo — Guildmaster

The young administrator holding the world's response together on no sleep — four days counted by his aide, the "I understand" diplomatic metronome, the half-second delayed bow to Lords as his only available rebellion. He humanizes the macro plot, and his Murim backstory ("Junior Brother" to Yoo) economically links continents. Critique: he vanishes for the entire back half of Book Two; for a character introduced this carefully, the series owes him either relevance or a graceful reduction. Grade: B.

Lumina — Void God, Rank 6

The youngest of the Ten, defined by the gap between her rank and her self-belief ("waiting for someone to figure out she didn't belong") — a sympathetic note that keeps the godscale cast human. Her rift-casting explanation is the best magic-system exposition in the book (Asriel's incredulity does the work), and her cracked mask is a strong unexplained image. Critique: after the insertion she evaporates into the Shog'Nai assignment; her imposter syndrome is set up but never tested on-page. Clearly a B3 character; fine, but the setup is now two books old. Grade: B.

Inarus Suma — The Apprentice

The late-arriving comic engine: scarred, nasally, self-titled future Mage God, horrified by book vandalism, allergic to divine casting's lack of "reproducible methodology." His chemistry with Muse (the bickering theology walk in ch. 37 — her "display animal in someone's collection" defense of divine non-intervention against his grief-shaped skepticism) instantly fills the banter vacuum the dissolved party left, which is precisely his structural job. The burn scars and the "people around me tend to come out worse than I do" line promise a real wound under the comedy. Critique: arrived too late to be tested; his missing teachers are an obvious, good engine — the text should beware making him only a Muse accessory. Grade: B+ (high potential).

The Merchant — The White Parka

The series' designated mystery: an unkillable salesman with a bottomless pack who walks through undead dungeons humming, sources apocalypse-grade artifacts (the dome artifact, the Soul Gem), charges in information and structural favors (every Lord naming a successor — the most ominous price in the book), performs free body disposal "for my new favorite customer," and resurrects Dantalion apparently for the fun of the revenge story. Shot always from outside, voice always bright, face always shadowed. The white parka — visually rhyming with Orsted's — is either a deliberate misdirection or an accident; the author should decide which. Critique: none yet — he is exactly as present as a mystery should be at this stage. The danger is only in the future: his client list (who bought the successor names? who gets the gem?) must eventually resolve into one coherent design. Grade: A− as a device.

Xeroc — The Watching God

The most quietly unsettling presence in Book Two: a god of no stated domain whose symbol is an eye, who arrives precisely when the prophet's despair peaks, whose warmth is genuine and whose terms ("find my Church, and we will speak again properly") are never not contractual. The text seeds doubt with surgical economy — Naika "fell" ("that's not the word I would use"); the gift of choice reframed as a marketplace; the all-clear that wasn't ("I'd keep going if I were you"). Whether Xeroc is benevolent, predatory, or something orthogonal is the series' best theological mystery. Critique: maintain the ration; one Xeroc scene per arc, never an answer without a new question. Grade: A− as a device.

Pope Lucas — The Natural Order

One chapter, fully realized: a shirtless tattooed elf alone in a frozen cathedral, every inch of skin inked with the names of everyone he killed in the Age of Corpses — "My sins are mine to bear." His joy at witnessing a real prophet ("equal parts reverence and desperation") makes him the warmest religious authority in the book and, given this series' track record with institutions, automatically suspicious. Grade: B+ for a single-chapter character.

Andromalius — Lord of Gold, Rank 72

The villain the series uses to teach the reader what the 72 are: entitlement with a bloodline, "I've never looked it up" as a thesis statement, a golden warhammer, a dragon in the basement, and human shields as combat doctrine. His arc is complete and well-shaped — beaten not by power but by two empty people refusing to fall, exiled with a survival pack, his last command ignored by a silent crowd (the quietest humiliation in the book), and his off-page death the political tripwire that brings Barbatos inside. Critique: his hammer-to-dragon escalation in ch. 11–12 stacks two reveals where one might breathe better. Grade: B+.

Jane — Calamity

Billy's right hand and the crew's actual brain: the mutiny-as-ritual ("She does this every couple of jobs… That's why she's my right hand"), the cold risk assessment that walks away from Solarspire, the instant recalibration when Barbatos liquefies one of her men. She's the person in Billy's orbit who survives by never believing him. Critique: functional, not yet dimensional — one scene of what she wants would pay dividends, especially as Carina enters the crew's gravity. Grade: B−.

Talia — The Lady Dantalion

One scene, maximal impact: the elf draped on a stolen throne who wanted to kill her own mother personally and is annoyed her husband did it first. As world-texture for the Dantalion household she's perfect; as a character she's a silhouette — and now a dangling succession grievance (the pact named Elizabeth precisely to disinherit her). Critique: either a B3 player or a cut; currently she's a loaded gun on a shelf no one is watching. Grade: C+ (incomplete).

Worth a Line

  • Thane Aldrich (B1 ch. 1): the survival-arithmetic thesis in one scene — "Then don't come back for our sake." Perfect cold open antagonist-of-circumstance.
  • The Crimson Port Harbormaster: "That's what the last group said too" — institutional exhaustion in one line; his reappearance at the dock flyby is a satisfying micro-payoff.
  • Count Dravemont: competent siege commander; correctly written as someone the party empowers rather than rescues.
  • Gudrun: two scenes, one function, lands the book's most important gift — "We all need a hand around here" — and receives its saddest thank-you.
  • Paimon (Rank 9): the only Lord who reads Barbatos accurately; vanished since B1 ch. 30 with the series' best unanswered warning ("He wants something inside the dome"). Bring her back.
  • Asriel, Kephri, Voss, Turdec, Castiel & Yara: the summit ensemble — efficient national-interest sketches; Asriel's "write it on the board" list is the best joke in the outside-world chapters.
  • The Wendigo: not a character, but the series' best monster — see the horror section.
