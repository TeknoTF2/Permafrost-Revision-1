Barbatos (Book One: "Barbados")

A philanthropist so old he's forgotten his own atrocities — the man who erased a religion and genuinely cannot remember why.

Role: The series' apex villain; Cain's defining hatred Origin: Lords of Medicine bloodline, inverted; centuries old Signature: Silver hair, purple cape, weaponized charm, touch-delivered death Grade: A

Who He Is

Barbatos is built on the manuscript's single best villain insight: the atrocity that defines the hero's life was, to its perpetrator, a Tuesday. Cain spends a thousand years underground sharpening one name; Barbatos meets him face to face and asks "Do I know you?" — and when finally cornered into explaining the genocide, produces the most chilling line in either book: "You probably just annoyed me." Evil as administrative forgetting. It reframes the entire revenge plot: there is no satisfying answer at the bottom of Cain's quest, because the enemy of his life never considered him a character in theirs.

The construction around that core is consistently smart. His public mode — the warm laugh, the humble-brag philanthropy, the "rehabilitation program" built from debt slaves he addicted himself — is rendered well enough that the reader feels the room lean toward him even while knowing better (the summit scene, where his framing visibly lands on people who know exactly what he is, is the best demonstration of charisma-as-weapon in the book). His private mode is colder and stranger: the inverted Lords-of-Medicine inheritance (every "cure" he markets is stolen; everything original he's made kills), the Skogsund kebab scene with its quiet "tastes practically just like pork" — a villain who recognizes cannibalism, tips generously, and moves on — and the bandit liquefaction delivered with the same warmth as the compliment to the chef. He is a man for whom sincerity and performance long ago became the same instrument; the text says it directly: "a mask worn so long it fit better than the face beneath it."

Arc & Function

Across two books he runs three games at once: the public benefactor angling resources at the dome crisis, the private operator hunting Dantalion's leverage (the Green Alkanet plot — the rare McGuffin chain where every link is character-driven), and the ancient predator following an itch in his perfect memory south to the ruins. The Dantalion throne-room duel (B2 ch. 14) — the series' best villain-on-villain scene — establishes both his physical superiority and his one vulnerability: a mind that can be led by its own logic. Dantalion beats him with a bluff built from true premises, and the loss visibly costs him something readers rarely see a top villain pay: certainty.

The finale repays everything. Stripped of audience, allies, and four spent contingency rings, he fights Cain in the church he burned — and the fight's choreography is a moral diagram: Barbatos's whole style is deferral (poison that wins later, barriers that spend now, simulacrums that promise resurrection), against an opponent whose style is refusing to defer anything. His death — throat torn by teeth, mid-poisoning, both men finishing each other on the nave floor — lands as the collision of the only two characters in the book who experience time at the same terrible scale. His exit claim ("I've got simulacrums across the planet… my soul will just transfer") keeps the door open; see critique.

Critique

  • The name. (Must fix.) "Barbados" throughout Book One, "Barbatos" throughout Book Two. Standardize to Barbatos (the Goetic spelling, consistent with Bael/Paimon/Dantalion/Andromalius/Vassago).
  • The simulacrum escape hatch needs rules now. If he returns, the finale's price must be visible and steep (memory loss mirrors Cain's grave-erosion beautifully; a body downgrade; the loss of the bloodline seal). If he doesn't return, the claim should be falsified on-page in B3 — otherwise it dangles as an un-cashed threat forever.
  • His power set is impressionistic. Touch-poison, contingency rings, a knife — fine; but the gap between "liquefies a man by looking at him" (Skogsund) and "loses a knife fight to attrition" (the ruins) is bridged only by inference (the first target was a nobody; Cain is unkillable). One line establishing what his poison can and cannot do to extraordinary bodies would close it.
  • Paimon, his designated foil, vanishes after B1. She's the person who reads him correctly and the natural outside-world thread for B3 — don't lose her.

Verdict — A. A top-shelf villain built from a single devastating idea — the banality of ancient evil — and executed with tonal control across charm, horror, and farce. The series' best line belongs to him. Fix the spelling, price the resurrection, and he's the antagonist this story deserves.
