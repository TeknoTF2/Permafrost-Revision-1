The Party · Paladin · The Wall

Cain

A satyr paladin who cannot die, sworn never to kill another human — carrying a vow, a prophet, and a name he's been waiting a thousand years to hear again.

Role: Frontline, commander, co-protagonist Origin: Soldier → Solar Covenant paladin; buried alive ~1,000 years Signature: Shield, halberd, one eye, hooves on stone, the vow Grade: A

Who He Is

Cain is the genre's stoic guardian archetype rebuilt with two radical modifications. First, his immortality is a horror, not a power fantasy: Book Two's opening chapter reveals he spent the thousand years not asleep but conscious — dying of asphyxiation, waking, digging, dying again, "hundreds of times. Thousands. Millions?" — dragging Muse's coffin upward through grave dirt. The text refuses to explain it (Fey Rule or Naika's grace; "Cain didn't care. He needed the meaning"), and that refusal is the character: a man who chose faith as an interpretive frame because the alternative was a meaningless eternity in the dark. Second, his pacifism is specific and load-bearing: a vow never to take another human life, made after a war where he "invented steam torture" — the nickname "Kettle," delivered by Barbatos in the finale, retroactively floods every previous restraint scene with new meaning. Cain holds back not because he is gentle but because he remembers exactly what he is when he doesn't.

Arc Map

Book One — The Vow Under Pressure

B1 tests the vow in ascending order. He insists on "his way first" with Andromalius (process as the thing "that separates us from what we just walked out of"), survives being the party's conscience through the assassination debate, and delivers the book's central ethical position against Carina in the dungeon ("killing isn't a decision you unmake… Who decides? Where does it stop?"). Then the book turns his own argument against him twice: Andromalius speaks the name Barbados and Cain — mid-speech about restraint — beats the Lord into the floor screaming, stopped only by a dragon; and in the aftermath, his alley breakdown (vomiting, the panic he believed had been "beaten out of him") shows the wall's interior for the first time. His resolution — exiling Andromalius with a survival pack, "It wasn't justice… This was just a man making a decision because someone had to" — is the most honest treatment of vigilante ethics in the book.

Book Two — The Erosion and the Fall

B2 dismantles him in three moves. The corridor scene (ch. 16) — Barbatos, ten feet away, asking "Do I know you?" — is exquisite cruelty: the man who destroyed everything doesn't remember doing it. The stairwell (ch. 30–31) reveals the cost of the grave that the resurrection chapter only implied: he has forgotten the scripture. "I don't remember the Vow of Protection… I've been reciting what I remember and filling in the gaps with what feels right." His faith, the thing that made the thousand years survivable, is a reconstruction — and the one person who holds the originals is leaving for other gods. His departure from the party is gentle, generous ("Carina. I always knew you'd come back. I'm proud of you"), and a lie of omission: he is not going to find churches. He is going to find Barbatos.

The finale (ch. 39) completes the arc with the manuscript's bravest beat. Asked why the Covenant was destroyed, Barbatos answers, "You probably just annoyed me." And asked — by Barbatos — what great reason drives him, Cain answers: "I don't have any good reason. I just hate you." The paladin's last act strips itself of every justification he spent two books constructing; he breaks the vow with his teeth, in the ruins of his own church, and dies (for now) beside his enemy. Whether read as tragic fall or as the only honest act available to him, it is the right ending for this arc — the book's two ethical poles (Cain's law, Carina's strength) collapse into the same grave.

Voice & Craft

Cain's POV runs on tactical inventory — reading the party as "a unit," cataloguing Uthesia's hit-and-run patterns mid-bear-fight, planning logistics during a hostage negotiation — which makes the moments his syntax breaks (the alley, the blindfold illusion, WHERE IS BARBADOS) hit like structural failures. His dialogue is a long-running deadpan engine ("I could leave you." / "You're a real conversationalist, you know that?") that doubles as armor; the book understands that the joke about Cain not talking is Cain not talking. Physical comedy through him (the drainage pipe, hooves on stairwells "like a blacksmith's hammer on an anvil") is deployed exactly often enough.

Key Scenes

  • B1 ch. 9: the alley breakdown — Barbados, the vow's origin, "a wall that cracks doesn't protect anything."
  • B1 ch. 12: the held-back strikes Carina reads; the berserk eruption when the name lands.
  • B1 ch. 22: exiling Andromalius; justice without a system.
  • B2 ch. 1: die, wake, dig — the grave chapter. Genre-defining backstory.
  • B2 ch. 16: "Do I know you?" — restraint as agony.
  • B2 ch. 26: the flamberge bite — teeth shattering on steel, holding until the heal. Pure Cain: endurance as offense.
  • B2 ch. 39: Kettle, the cliff, the bite, the mutual death. The series' best chapter.

Critique

  • The immortality needs a posted price. Two books in, dying has cost Cain memory (excellent) but the rules are otherwise opaque. If ch. 39's death is reversible, the reversal must spend something visible, or every future Cain jeopardy is discounted.
  • His decision to hunt Barbatos alone is under-dramatized at the moment of choice. The reader infers it; the party doesn't; even his POV slides past the decision itself. One paragraph of him choosing — and knowing what he's choosing — would sharpen the tragedy.
  • "Kettle" / steam torture is a magnificent reveal that arrives with no earlier shadow. A single image seeded in B1 (steam, kettles, an averted memory in the alley chapter — which already gestures at "rooms of steam") would convert shock into devastation. Note: B1 ch. 12 does mention "people in rooms of steam" — so the seed exists; it should be watered once more.
  • Early-book invincibility calibration: he survives a golden warhammer through two stone walls in ch. 11 before the reader knows he can't die; some readers will file it as plot armor until B2 ch. 1 retroactively explains. Consider a hint earlier.

Relationships

Muse: his purpose, explicitly — "Naika was Muse, and Muse was asleep by the fire… that was enough." The schism works because both are right. Carina: the book's central ethical debate, conducted across two volumes, ending with him adopting her position and her adopting his grief. "I'm proud of you" is the last kind thing anyone says to her before the collapse — which makes it devastating. Gormund: the two veterans; their craft-talk about a cracked halberd shaft (B1 ch. 5) is quiet gold. Uthesia: "You're the wall, I'm the blade" — the partnership of the two people who volunteer for the dark. Barbatos: the defining hatred; see above.

Verdict — A. The most archetypal party member on the surface and the most extreme underneath. His arc has the cleanest tragic geometry in the series — vow, erosion, fall — and the grave chapter plus the finale are portfolio pieces. The revision's job is to price his immortality and to seed Kettle's shadow earlier.
