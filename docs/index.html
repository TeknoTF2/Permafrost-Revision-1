The Short Version

Permafrost is the real thing. Underneath a familiar premise — a fantasy nation sealed under a dome of apocalyptic winter — is a two-book story with an unusually disciplined emotional core: five broken strangers who become a family, and then a second book brave enough to break that family apart, piece by piece, for reasons that are each individually earned. The blind prophet's POV is genuinely distinctive craft. The fight choreography runs on tactics and cost rather than power levels. The villains scheme against each other as much as against the heroes, which keeps the antagonist half of the book alive in a way most fantasy never manages. And the series' best chapters — the campfire wishes, the knock on the cabin door, Muse screaming at an empty heaven, Carina's dream of the House of Murder, Cain's teeth on the flamberge, the final mutual kill in the ruined church — are the kind of scenes readers screenshot and send to friends.

What holds it back from being ready right now: Book One ends without a self-contained climax (it stops rather than lands); the inside-the-dome and outside-the-dome casts compete for the spine of the story; a layer of line-level issues (comma splices, tense slips, the Barbados/Barbatos spelling drift, inconsistent thought-punctuation) needs a copyedit pass; a handful of continuity contradictions (Carina's trial age, cultivation rank names) need reconciling; and — most seriously for any commercial path — two characters are recognizably borrowed from an existing licensed property and must be renamed and redesigned before this can be queried, serialized for money, or published. All of this is fixable. None of it touches the heart of what works.

What This Story Is Actually About

The dome is the premise, but it isn't the subject. Across seventy chapters, Permafrost keeps returning to one question asked five different ways: what do you hold onto when the thing that defined you goes silent? Muse loses her goddess; Cain loses his scripture; Carina loses (and is lost by) both of her families; Gormund loses the road that made him more than a production line; Uthesia loses the family she built and nearly becomes the weapon she ran from. Every major antagonist is a dark mirror of the same question — Andromalius clings to a bloodline he never earned, Dantalion to an inheritance he never wanted, Barbatos to a self-image so old he can't remember why he destroyed a religion. Even the title works on this level: things preserved by the cold, intact but frozen, waiting to thaw into either life or rot.

That coherence is the manuscript's single greatest asset, and it's why the brutal ending of Book Two — the party scattering, each member relapsing into the exact wound the journey had been healing — reads as tragic design rather than authorial cruelty. The full argument is on the Story & Craft page.

Five Things That Are Working at a Professional Level

1. Muse's blind POV

The sensory writing in Muse's chapters — counting steps, reading hesitation in footfalls, navigating surgery by touch, hearing a smile — is consistently inventive without ever being a gimmick. The blind-surgery sequence in Book Two ch. 8 is the kind of chapter that sells a series.

2. Fights that run on problem-solving

Every major fight has a tactical idea: the dire bear's hide that nothing penetrates, the hammer in the dragon's throat, the Angel's windup/winddown rhythm, Ghislaine's prediction-illusions, Stonewalker's antimagic sting. Combat is never just damage exchange — it's argumentation by violence.

3. The villains' second front

Dantalion vs. Barbatos vs. Elizabeth vs. the Merchant is a full political thriller running underneath the survival story, and the manuscript's smartest twist — Dantalion engineering his own murder and resurrection — lands because the family's dynamic was built with care for fifteen chapters first.

4. Earned sentiment

The campfire promise in Book One ch. 6 ("when the dome breaks…") is planted early, paid off in fragments, and weaponized at the end of Book Two ("Muse, we were going to see the sun"). This is long-game emotional architecture, and it works.

5. Tonal range under control

The same book contains the Wendigo's polite knock (genuine horror), Cain stuck in a drainage pipe (genuine comedy), and Elizabeth's "I love you too, Dad" (genuine horror again, differently). The tonal gear-shifts almost always land because they're anchored in character.

Bonus: Gormund

The pessimist-craftsman who narrates survival as arithmetic is the easiest character to love and the hardest kind to write. His relapse chapter (B2 ch. 36) is the quietest and maybe truest chapter in either book.

Five Things That Need Work Before Anything Else

1. The IP problem (blocking)

Orsted and Ghislaine are recognizable characters from Mushoku Tensei in name, design, and role. For private fanfiction this is fine; for any commercial or public-serial path they must be renamed and meaningfully redesigned. Details on the Market page.

2. Book One's ending

Book One resolves Valdara at ch. 22 and then runs eight chapters of (excellent) escalation that belongs to Book Two's spine. It ends on setup, not climax. Either restructure so the Wendigo or Valdara serves as the book-one capstone, or embrace web-serial structure where this matters less.

3. The outside-the-dome cast arrives late and heavy

Chapters 19–20 of Book One drop ~15 named characters, three factions, and two ranking systems in two chapters, mid-crisis, with the party offscreen. The information is good; the delivery needs distribution.

4. Line-level cleanup

Comma splices, tense wobbles, inconsistent inner-thought punctuation (single quotes vs. italics), the Barbados/Barbatos spelling drift between books, and a handful of garbled sentences (the maid's death in B1 ch. 11 most critically). A full copyedit pass is needed; specifics in the Beta Read.

5. Continuity reconciliation

Carina's House of Murder trial age is 12–13 in Book One and 9 in Book Two; cultivation rank names shift; the inside/outside timeline is fuzzy; B1's lone chapter epigraph (ch. 23) is an orphaned device. Small fixes, big credibility gains.

Bonus: Power-scaling guardrails

Yoo and Orsted are written as functional gods, which Book Two manages by keeping them busy elsewhere — but the series needs a durable answer for why top-tier characters can't simply solve each arc. The dome's suppression is a good start; make the rules visible and binding.
