The Read, As Experienced

I read both books in order, cover to cover, over a continuous sitting-equivalent. Honest engagement curve: hooked by chapter 1 (the axe, the lie); fully invested by chapter 6 (the campfire wishes — this is where the party became people I was worried about); peak Book One immersion through the Valdara arc (ch. 8–18, where I stopped taking notes for stretches because I forgot to); a real stall at ch. 19–20 (the outside-world pivot — I checked how many pages remained, which is the tell); recovery through the Wendigo arc; and a Book One ending that left me motivated for Book Two but aware I hadn't gotten a finale.

Book Two held me more consistently: the grave chapter is a stunning re-entry, the ship arc is pure pleasure, the Ostervik siege is the best sustained sequence in either book, and the final five chapters had me bracing on every page-turn. The dissolution sequence upset me at the characters rather than at the author — the distinction that separates earned tragedy from manuscript problems — with one exception noted below (the speed of Gormund and Muse's street exits). I finished Book Two at the kiss, sat back, and would have immediately opened Book Three. That is the single most important data point in this report.

What Kept Me Turning Pages

  • The party's company. I wanted to be at their campfires. Banter quality is the #1 retention device in found-family fantasy, and this manuscript has it in the top tier. Specific addictions: any Gormund/Carina exchange; Cain's deadpan; Uthesia's rationed one-liners.
  • Chapter-ending discipline. "Tweet. Tweet." — the hooves on wood that aren't Cain — "She's in there." — "Muse didn't answer." This author understands the page-turn as a unit of craft.
  • The dread economy. Threats are introduced long before they arrive (the dragging sound runs five chapters before the knock; the golem hums two chapters before it fires). I was never ambushed by an unearned escalation.
  • Competence rendered honestly. The surgery, the furnace, the siege logistics — I trust this book when it tells me how something works, and that trust transfers to caring whether it works.
  • Dramatic irony done right. Carina passing the warship her family is aboard; the party hauling the Lords' McGuffin as deck weeds; the reader knowing Billy while Carina can't afford to. The book lets me know more than the characters and makes that knowledge hurt.

Where I Stumbled

  • B1 ch. 19's cold plunge. Fifteen names in one chapter directly after the emotional peak. I had to flip back twice to re-place Voss vs. Turdec. (Fix: distribute earlier, or trust readers to meet the council later.)
  • The maid's death sentence (B1 ch. 11). I read the paragraph three times to determine the physical sequence at the most important moment in Book One. It must be unambiguous.
  • Inner-thought formatting. The single-quote convention intermittently breaks (unpaired quotes, mid-thought switches), and in dense scenes I occasionally attributed a thought to the wrong character for a line.
  • The Barbados→Barbatos shift. I genuinely paused in B2 ch. 7 to check whether a new character had been introduced.
  • B2 ch. 32–33. The one stretch where my attention was earned by goodwill rather than the page. Trimmable.
  • Bracketed spell-names ([MASS GREATER HEALING]). Effective in the moment, but appearing only twice and only in Book Two, they read as an imported convention from web fiction; standardize or stylize.

Things I Didn't Quite Buy

  • Muse's surgery succeeding at the level described. Bowel sutures through fist-width wounds, blind, first exposure to the tools — I allowed it because the chapter's process writing is so committed, but I noticed myself allowing it. One acknowledgment that it shouldn't have worked (Orsted's "ninety percent" helps a lot — lean on it) buys the rest.
  • The Solarspire heist's risk calculus (B2 ch. 15–17). Carina solo-infiltrating a castle hosting two Lords, a Ten Great Power on patrol, and the Royal Guard — knowing Ghislaine exists — reads as recklessness even for her. The text needs one beat of her accounting for Ghislaine and being wrong, rather than not accounting at all.
  • Yoo redirected by Dantalion (B2 ch. 12). The Vassago lever is clever, but the strongest person in the story leaves a throne room containing a fresh corpse on an unverified story. Her urgency about Carina motivates it — put that motivation on the page in that scene.
  • Yoo dying (?) to the choke (B2 ch. 40). I believe she chose not to resist; I don't yet believe her body couldn't passively survive it. Cultivation rules earlier established her as bullet-proof-adjacent. If she's dead, I need one line of mechanism (releasing her reinforcement; meeting the choke with open meridians — anything). If she's alive, fine — but the pulse-check misdirection will feel like a cheat to part of the audience either way. Decide and pay.
  • The party letting Carina walk out alone in B1 ch. 18–23, twice. The first time (injured, furious, into a plague city at night) is the plot's hinge and gets surprisingly little on-page resistance from Muse, the person we're told would resist most. Her later guilt acknowledges it; the moment itself could carry more friction.
  • Hallowed Ground's "no casualties" framing. Discussed elsewhere — the vow's absolutism invites a score-keeping the text doesn't intend.

Confusion Points

  • Timeline, inside vs. outside: 22 days of divine silence (B1 ch. 1) vs. "eight weeks since the dome" (B1 ch. 19) — reconcilable with travel time, but I did arithmetic mid-chapter, which is never the goal. One dated anchor per act fixes it.
  • Cain's pre-dome wake-up window: he surfaces, sits "a day, maybe a few weeks," then the dome falls and hunger returns. The causality (Naika's protection ending at the dome) is suggestive but fuzzy — deliberate mystery or drafting artifact? Flag for intent.
  • The Wendigo hearing silent ki-link instructions (B1 ch. 27): the text notices the problem ("it heard… wait, it never—") and drops it. Either it reads minds (escalate the horror!) or the scene needs Uthesia speaking aloud.
  • Who knew what about the Green Alkanet: by B2's end, Fafnir knows the ship's cargo, Ghislaine searched it, Elizabeth explained its meaning, and Barbatos believes the samples were a bluff — but the physical weeds sit docked at the northern crossing with no one visibly moving toward them. A one-line status would prevent readers from thinking the book forgot its own McGuffin. (I'm 90% sure it hasn't — Ghislaine's search is the tell — but say so.)
  • Uthesia's summon inventory: "dozens" pre-series → 2 flame elementals in the cave → "six small ones" on the ship → a brigade by Ostervik (captures explain growth, but losses are easy to lose track of). A reader-facing rule of thumb (her counting habit could carry it) would help.
  • Murim ranking vocabulary: Third Star, Advanced, Saint, Great/Sacred Transcendent, Soul Palace — at least three scales in play. I built a mental table and was never sure it was right.

Line-Level Patterns (with Examples)

This draft needs one full copyedit pass. The issues are patterned, which makes them efficient to fix:

  • Unintentional comma splices: "The log split clean, she could tell by the way the two halves tumbled apart…" (B1 ch. 1). The style earns splices in action; in calm narration they read as errors. Rule of thumb: keep them in fights and panic, fix them in description.
  • Broken thought-quote pairing: "'It's been weeks,' Muse thought… And Naika…'" (B1 ch. 1) — opening/closing marks mismatch repeatedly across both books. Recommend converting all direct thought to italics and abolishing the quote convention entirely.
  • Agreement/tense slips under momentum: "when you live in a place where the enemies at your gates every few months" (B1 ch. 5); scattered POV-adjacent slips in long sentences.
  • Garbled action at high-stakes moments: the B1 ch. 11 maid sentence (priority #1); the B2 ch. 13 wagon flash-cuts; a few mid-fight referent blurs ("it/he" chains across three combatants).
  • Construction tics to ration: "the kind of X that Y" (several per chapter at peak); "something that was not quite A but was adjacent to B"; bodies/brains "doing the math"; "the [noun] of a [person] who [had done something]" appositive stacks. All good tools — currently overdrawn.
  • Emotion double-tap: action shows a feeling, following clause names it ("…and the flinch broke whatever held him and the fear was visible"-type doubling). Cut the label, keep the behavior.
  • Scene-break asterisk formatting varies (`* * *` with inconsistent spacing/indentation) — normalize in layout.
  • Typos noted in passing (non-exhaustive): "the enemies at your gates" (verb missing, B1 ch. 5); "Östervik" with umlaut in B1 ch. 5 vs. "Ostervik" elsewhere; "PERMAFROST" end-tags vary between "PERMAFROST" and "PERMAFROST — Book Two" (production artifact, presumably); occasional doubled spaces and inconsistent em-dash/hyphen usage.

Content Notes

For the package (publisher or serial front-matter): graphic violence including violence against civilians; child death (the trials, off-page but explicit); animal death; suicide-adjacent self-destruction ideation; alcoholism and relapse; grooming/predatory relationship dynamics involving a 19-year-old (written critically, never glamorized — but readers deserve the flag); body horror; cannibalism implication; parental abuse and abandonment. The book handles all of these with intent; the note is about reader consent, not censorship.

Questions I'd Ask the Author

  1. Is Yoo alive? (Don't tell me — tell the page, in Book Three's first act.)
  2. Is the Merchant's parka an Orsted misdirect, a connection, or a coincidence?
  3. Was "…go…" Naika's last word, Xeroc's first, or a seed for the epicenter? It's the series' oldest open loop.
  4. Who bought the successor names, and who receives the Soul Gem? (Same buyer? The symmetry is suspicious in the best way.)
  5. What does Hallowed Ground's eye see? Cain's dread implies Xeroc's sigil means something specific to a thousand-year-old paladin — does he know more than the text has said?
  6. Does Carina know the maid's name? (If Book Three ever gives it to her, I will need to lie down.)
  7. What is Skogsund eating, exactly — and does Barbatos's silence about it have a price?
  8. Is Book One's non-ending a serial-format choice or an oversight? The answer determines half the structural revision.

Revision Priorities, Ranked

Blocking (do before anything public/commercial)
  1. Rename/redesign the two borrowed characters (see Market).
  2. Full copyedit pass (thought-punctuation convention, splices, the ch. 11 maid sentence).
  3. Barbados→Barbatos standardization; Carina timeline reconciliation; cultivation-rank glossary.
Structural (next revision)
  1. Give Book One an ending (Wendigo death relocated, or equivalent capstone).
  2. Distribute the outside-world introduction (B1 ch. 19–20) across earlier interludes.
  3. Commit on Yoo's fate; add the one-beat costs in the dissolution (Gormund/Muse noticing Carina); reposition or counterweight the final Billy scene with the Gormund ember.
  4. Tighten B2 ch. 32–33; trim the Guild-benefits and Murim-civics speeches.
Polish (ongoing)
  1. Ration the construction tics; cut emotion-labels after shown behavior.
  2. Post the rules: Uthesia's soul costs, Cain's death price, tremor-sense limits, spell-bracket convention.
  3. Seed Gormund's drinking and Kettle's steam earlier; one more Billy dimension; faces for Uthesia's dead.
